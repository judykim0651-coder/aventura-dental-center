Cracked tooth symptoms can be subtle at first. A tooth may hurt only when you bite in a certain way, or feel sensitive to cold without a clear reason. Because symptoms vary and do not confirm a crack on their own, a dental evaluation is the way to understand what may be happening.

## What can a cracked tooth feel like?

A crack can produce several kinds of discomfort. Some people notice a sudden, sharp pain when chewing or when they release their bite. Others have occasional aching or sensitivity to hot or cold foods and drinks. The sensation may come and go, which can make it difficult to identify the tooth or the cause.

Common signs to notice include:

* Pain when biting down, especially on a particular tooth or at a particular angle.
* A sharp twinge as you release a bite.
* Unexplained sensitivity to cold or hot foods and liquids.
* Discomfort that appears during chewing but is not constant.
* Swelling or tenderness near a tooth, which can have multiple possible causes and deserves professional attention.

Not every crack causes pain, and these symptoms can also occur with other dental problems. A crack may not be visible in a mirror, and the absence of a visible line does not rule it out. Avoid trying to diagnose the problem by repeatedly biting down to reproduce pain.

## What can trigger symptoms?

Chewing puts pressure on teeth, so a cracked tooth may hurt when it meets food or when pressure changes as you release your bite. Hard or crunchy foods can make discomfort more noticeable. Temperature changes may also bring on sensitivity. These triggers are clues to report, not a reliable way to identify the size or location of a crack.

Note what you were doing when the pain began, which foods or temperatures seem to affect it, and whether discomfort is getting more frequent. Share these observations with the dentist. Do not intentionally test the tooth with hard foods or extreme temperatures; that can worsen discomfort without clarifying the cause.

## When should you seek dental care?

Arrange a dental evaluation if you have recurring pain on biting, unexplained temperature sensitivity, or discomfort that keeps returning. A prompt call is especially sensible after a tooth injury or when symptoms are changing. A dentist can assess the tooth and discuss next steps based on the examination.

Call a dental office for guidance if you have significant pain, swelling, or a broken tooth. If swelling is severe or you have trouble breathing or swallowing, seek emergency medical care. Do not wait for a routine appointment when symptoms feel urgent.

Until you receive guidance, avoid chewing on the sore side and choose softer foods that do not provoke pain. Keep the area clean with gentle brushing. Do not put aspirin or other medication directly on the tooth or gum, and do not use household glue or attempt to repair a crack yourself. If you need pain relief, follow the product label and ask a pharmacist or clinician if you are unsure whether a medicine is safe for you.

## How does a dentist evaluate a possible crack?

A dental visit usually begins with your symptoms and health history. The dentist may ask when discomfort started, whether it occurs with biting or temperature, and whether there was an injury. An examination may include looking at the tooth and surrounding gums, checking how the bite comes together, and assessing sensitivity. Imaging may be used when needed, although a crack is not always straightforward to see on an image.

Depending on the findings, the dentist may use additional clinical checks to help locate the source of symptoms. A crack can be hard to diagnose because symptoms may be intermittent and overlap with other conditions. The appropriate next step depends on the tooth, the extent and position of any damage, and your individual circumstances. A dental professional can explain the findings and options after evaluation.

The [review of cracked tooth syndrome in the National Library of Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8694987/) describes pain on biting and cold sensitivity among reported symptoms. It also emphasizes that clinical presentation can vary, so a symptom list is not a substitute for an examination.

## What should you do while waiting?

* Choose soft foods and avoid chewing on the affected side if that reduces discomfort.
* Avoid hard, sticky, or very hot or cold foods and drinks if they trigger symptoms.
* Keep up gentle oral hygiene; do not poke at the tooth or gum.
* Write down when pain occurs and any recent injury, then share that information at the appointment.
* Call for professional guidance if symptoms worsen or you develop swelling.

These steps may help you avoid provoking symptoms, but they do not treat or confirm a crack. Do not postpone an evaluation simply because the pain comes and goes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a cracked tooth hurt only sometimes?

Yes. Some cracked teeth cause intermittent discomfort, such as pain during a particular bite or sensitivity to temperature. Symptoms alone cannot establish whether a tooth is cracked, so recurring episodes are worth discussing with a dentist.

### Can I see a crack myself?

Not necessarily. Some cracks are difficult to see without a dental examination, and a visible line does not by itself show how deep or significant the damage is. Avoid scraping or pressing on the area to investigate it.

### Does pain on biting always mean a crack?

No. Pain on biting can have different causes. A dentist can consider your symptoms, examine the tooth, and decide whether further checks or imaging are appropriate.

### Should I wait if the pain goes away?

Temporary relief does not identify the cause or show that a tooth is healthy. If pain returns, sensitivity persists, or you have swelling or a recent injury, contact a dental office for advice.
