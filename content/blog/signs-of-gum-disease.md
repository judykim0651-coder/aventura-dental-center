Knowing the signs of gum disease can help you act before a minor concern becomes a more serious oral health problem. Gums that bleed, look swollen, or pull away from the teeth deserve attention, even when there is little or no pain. **If you notice changes in your gums, schedule a dental evaluation so the cause can be assessed.**

## What are the common signs of gum disease?

Gum disease, also called periodontal disease, is an infection and inflammation of the tissues that support the teeth. It can begin with gingivitis, an early stage that may improve with professional care and consistent daily cleaning. Without attention, it may progress and affect deeper supporting tissues.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists several possible warning signs, including red, swollen, or tender gums and bleeding during brushing or flossing. You can review its patient guidance on [gum disease symptoms and prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/oral-health/about/gum-periodontal-disease.html).

* **Bleeding gums:** Blood on a toothbrush, floss, or in the sink can indicate irritation and should not simply be dismissed as normal.
* **Red, puffy, or tender gums:** Healthy gums generally do not remain sore or visibly inflamed.
* **Gums pulling away from teeth:** Receding gums can make teeth appear longer and may expose sensitive areas.
* **Persistent bad breath or an unpleasant taste:** These changes can sometimes be associated with bacteria and gum inflammation.
* **Loose or shifting teeth:** Movement may signal that the structures supporting a tooth need prompt assessment.
* **Changes in the way teeth fit together:** A new bite change or spaces between teeth can be worth discussing with a dentist.

One symptom alone does not confirm gum disease. Bleeding, tenderness, or bad breath can have more than one possible cause, so an examination is the safest way to understand what is happening.

## When should you see a dentist?

Make a routine dental appointment if bleeding continues, gum swelling returns, or your mouth feels different despite brushing and cleaning between your teeth. It is also sensible to arrange an evaluation when you notice persistent bad breath, gum recession, sensitivity near the gumline, or a tooth that feels less stable.

Seek prompt dental guidance for significant swelling, severe pain, pus, fever, difficulty swallowing, or difficulty opening your mouth. A broken or loose tooth after an injury also needs timely attention. If swelling affects breathing or swallowing, seek emergency medical care.

Do not wait for pain before asking for help. Gum disease may develop gradually, and some people have few noticeable symptoms in its earlier stages. A dental professional can help determine whether the change is related to gum inflammation, plaque buildup, tooth decay, trauma, grinding, or another concern.

## How is gum disease evaluated?

A dental evaluation usually starts with questions about your symptoms, medical history, medicines, and home-care routine. The dentist or dental team may look for redness, swelling, bleeding, recession, deposits on the teeth, and changes in tooth stability. They may also measure the spaces around the teeth and recommend imaging when it is needed to evaluate supporting bone.

This process helps distinguish early gum inflammation from more advanced periodontal concerns. It also helps create a plan based on your findings rather than on a generic treatment list. Depending on the evaluation, care may include improved brushing and interdental cleaning, professional cleaning, follow-up measurements, or additional treatment and monitoring.

Tell the dental team about bleeding, sensitivity, dry mouth, tobacco use, diabetes, pregnancy, or medicines that affect your mouth or bleeding risk. These details can help guide a safer, more complete assessment.

## What can help prevent gum problems?

Daily habits support healthy gums, but they do not replace professional examinations. Brush twice a day with a soft-bristled toothbrush and fluoride toothpaste. Clean between your teeth each day with floss, an interdental brush, or another method recommended for your mouth. Use gentle pressure: brushing harder is not a substitute for thorough cleaning.

Keep regular dental visits based on your individual needs. A professional can remove deposits that home cleaning cannot reach and can identify changes before they become more difficult to manage. Replace a worn toothbrush or brush head, and mention ongoing dry mouth or difficulty cleaning crowded areas so the team can suggest practical adjustments.

A balanced routine also includes avoiding tobacco products and following advice for health conditions that can affect the mouth. Mouthwash may be useful for some people, but it should complement—not replace—brushing, cleaning between teeth, and dental care.

## What should you do if you are worried about your gums?

Write down when the change began, whether bleeding occurs during brushing or flossing, and whether you have pain, swelling, bad breath, sensitivity, or tooth movement. Bring that information to your appointment. Photos can also help you describe a change that comes and goes, although they cannot replace an examination.
