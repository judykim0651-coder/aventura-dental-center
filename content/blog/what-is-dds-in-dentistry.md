## What is DDS in dentistry, in plain words?

D.D.S. is short for Doctor of Dental Surgery. It is the professional degree a person earns by graduating from dental school. You will see it printed after a dentist's name on a website, a card, or a sign. It is a credential about education, not a menu of services.

The word "surgery" is the part that confuses people. It sounds as if every D.D.S. spends the day in an operating room. That is not what the title means. Dental schools have used that wording for a long time. A dentist with this degree may provide checkups, fillings, crowns, and other general care, or may later complete extra training. The initials alone do not show which of those paths the person took.

People also mix up the degree with the name of the office. A practice can offer [family dentistry care](https://aventuradentalcenter.com/family-dentistry) for teens, adults, and seniors without that phrase appearing in the dentist's degree. Family dentistry is a way of organizing visits at one office. D.D.S. is the dentist's diploma. You can care about both, and they answer different questions.

The American Dental Association's MouthHealthy site says a D.D.S. and a D.M.D. both mean the dentist graduated from an accredited dental school. The same page notes that becoming a general dentist generally takes three or more years of college plus four years of dental school. After school, a dentist must pass a national written exam and a state or regional clinical exam before practicing. [Read the ADA explanation of DDS and DMD](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/dds-and-dmd).

## How is a D.D.S. different from a D.M.D.?

D.M.D. stands for Doctor of Dental Medicine, and some schools use Doctor of Medicine in Dentistry for the same letters. In the United States the two degrees are equivalent. The school, not the student, chooses which title appears on the diploma. The curriculum requirements are the same.

That is why the letters are a poor way to rank two dentists. A D.D.S. is not "more surgical" than a D.M.D. A D.M.D. is not "more medical." Skill, judgment, and the care a person is ready to discuss come from training and experience after the degree, plus the exam in front of them. If you are comparing offices, ask about the visit and the treatment, not the abbreviation.

## Does a D.D.S. mean the dentist is a specialist?

No. A D.D.S. or D.M.D. is the degree required to become a dentist. A specialty is extra education after that degree, in an area the profession has formally recognized. MouthHealthy states that additional post-graduate training is required to become a dental specialist, and it gives orthodontics, periodontics, and oral and maxillofacial surgery as examples.

The National Commission on Recognition of Dental Specialties and Certifying Boards currently recognizes 12 dental specialties. Recognition is meant for areas where the advanced knowledge and skills go beyond what a pre-doctoral dental program teaches. The Commission also says not every area of interest in dentistry meets that bar. A focus, a study club, or a weekend course is not the same thing as a recognized specialty.

The recognized specialties are dental anesthesiology, dental public health, endodontics, oral and maxillofacial pathology, oral and maxillofacial radiology, oral and maxillofacial surgery, oral medicine, orofacial pain, orthodontics and dentofacial orthopedics, pediatric dentistry, periodontics, and prosthodontics. You can [review the recognized dental specialties](https://ncrdscb.ada.org/recognized-dental-specialties) on the Commission's site. If a credential matters to your decision, ask what it is called and who issued it.

Board certification is a further step some specialists take through a recognized certifying board. It is not printed inside the letters D.D.S. Do not treat a degree, a specialty program, and board certification as the same claim. A general dentist can still be the right person for many everyday needs. When a case needs a different kind of training, that dentist can say so and help you understand the next step.

## What does the degree not tell you?

Patients often expect the letters to answer practical questions. They do not. A degree does not name the dentist who will examine you. It does not list the procedures that dentist offers. It does not set a fee, a timeline, or a result. Those answers come from a conversation and, when treatment is on the table, from an exam.

Licensure is separate from the diploma. MouthHealthy notes that a graduate must pass written and clinical licensing exams to practice, and must keep meeting continuing-education requirements to stay licensed. A degree from years ago does not, by itself, prove a current license in Florida. If you want to confirm a license, ask the office which board to check and use that official source.

## What can you ask before you book?

A short list keeps the first call useful. You do not need to quiz anyone. You do need to know whether this office is the right place for the concern you have.

1. Ask what the initials stand for, and whether the dentist is a D.D.S. or a D.M.D.
2. Ask who will examine you and whether that is the same person who would provide treatment.
3. Ask what training relates to the care you are considering, and whether any specialty credential is involved.
4. Ask which parts of care stay in the office and which might involve another clinician.
5. Ask what the exam needs to cover before anyone can talk about options, timing, or cost.

Bring the question in your own words. "I saw a D.D.S. after the dentist's name. Who would look at my concern, and is extra specialty training part of this?" is a fair question. So is "Will the dentist I meet be the person who does the treatment?" The office should be able to answer without treating the degree as a promise.

## What should you remember about a D.D.S.?

D.D.S. means Doctor of Dental Surgery. D.M.D. means Doctor of Dental Medicine. In the United States they are the same kind of dental degree, chosen by the school. Neither title means the dentist is a specialist, and neither title chooses your treatment.

Use the initials to confirm you are looking at a dentist. Then ask who will see you, what training fits the care you want, and what an exam still has to answer. That is the useful part of the credential conversation.

## Where do these letters show up?

You will see D.D.S. or D.M.D. in more places than a diploma. The same letters can appear on a website bio, a Google listing, a referral note, an insurance directory, and the sign in the reception area. Each place is copying a credential. None of those placements adds a new meaning. If one listing says D.D.S. and another shortens it to DDS, that is formatting, not a different degree.

Directory sites sometimes add their own labels, such as "cosmetic dentist" or "implant dentist." Those phrases can describe services an office wants patients to find. They are not the same as the 12 recognized specialties. A general dentist may provide cosmetic treatment or implant treatment after the right training and case selection. The listing still does not prove that you are a candidate, or that a specialty board certified the clinician.

When two sources disagree, ask the office which version is current. A bio might mention a school, a year, or a course. That is useful background. It is still background. The person who will examine you can explain what the training was, how recent it is, and whether it applies to your concern.

## How should you use the degree when you compare dentists?

Start by confirming the basics, then stop using the letters as a score. Both dentists finished dental school if one is a D.D.S. and the other is a D.M.D. The comparison that matters is the one you can verify: who you will see, what that person does often, and how the office explains choices.

Ask for the same facts from each office. Who does the exam? What records might be needed? What training relates to the treatment you asked about? If a specialty is part of the answer, what is the exact credential? If another clinician might be involved, when would that happen? Matching questions make the answers easier to compare than matching abbreviations.

Experience is not a number you should invent from a website, either. A long career can mean a dentist has seen many kinds of problems. It does not mean every newer treatment is offered, and it does not make an older plan outdated. At this practice, the team page attributes more than 37 years of local experience to Dr. Bistritz and says he continues to take courses. Treat that as the practice's account of its founder. Ask how it applies to the care you want.

Cost and coverage belong in that same conversation, not in the degree. Plans differ. A procedure that is common is not automatically a procedure your plan pays for. The front desk can ask what you are looking for and how you expect to pay, then schedule a visit. Nobody can quote a personal fee, a benefit, or a timeline from the letters D.D.S.

If you are early in the search, keep the decision small. Learn what the degree means. Confirm you are comfortable with the office's scope. Book a visit if you want an exam. Leave treatment choices, including implants, veneers, aligners, or a referral, until a dentist has looked at your mouth and explained the options in plain language.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is DDS in dentistry?

DDS, usually written D.D.S., means Doctor of Dental Surgery. It is the dental degree a person earns by graduating from an accredited dental school. It is not the name of a specialty.

### Is a D.M.D. the same as a D.D.S.?

Yes. The American Dental Association says both degrees mean the dentist graduated from an accredited dental school, with the same curriculum requirements. The university decides which title to award.

### Does D.D.S. mean the dentist performs surgery?

No. "Surgery" is part of the historic degree name. It does not mean the dentist only performs surgery, and it does not list the procedures that dentist offers.

### How can I tell whether a dentist is a specialist?

Ask for the specialty name and the organization that recognizes it. A D.D.S. or D.M.D. does not show specialty training. In the United States, the National Commission recognizes 12 dental specialties, and each one requires education beyond dental school.

### Can the letters D.D.S. tell me the cost or the result?

No. Fees, timing, and results depend on an exam and a plan for that person. A degree cannot promise coverage, a price, or an outcome.
