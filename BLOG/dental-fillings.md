## What Is a Dental Filling?

**Content Wireframe:**

- Definition: A dental filling is a restorative material used to repair a tooth damaged by decay, fracture, or wear, restoring its function and shape.
- Historical context: earliest fillings (7,000 BC — beeswax), amalgam (1819), composites (1960s), modern adhesive systems (1990s+).
- How fillings work: decay removed → cavity disinfected → filling material placed in layers → shaped and polished.
- Restorative spectrum: Filling (small) → Inlay/Onlay (medium) → Crown (large).

> "A filling isn't just plugging a hole. It's a precision restoration that must seal the tooth, withstand bite forces, and match natural aesthetics — all at once."

- **Visual suggestion:** Cross-section diagram of a tooth showing healthy enamel, dentin, decay, and the filled restoration (placeholder: 500×400).

## How Decay Develops: The Cavity Timeline

**Content Wireframe:**

```
STAGE 1: Demineralization
    └─ White spot lesion (reversible with fluoride)
STAGE 2: Enamel decay
    └─ Small cavity in enamel (requires filling)
STAGE 3: Dentin decay
    └─ Sensitivity, larger cavity (filling or possible root canal)
STAGE 4: Pulp involvement
    └─ Pain, abscess (root canal or extraction)
```

- Each stage described in 2–3 sentences.
- Emphasis on STAGE 1 as the window for reversal.
- **Visual suggestion:** 4-step progression graphic showing tooth decay stages (placeholder: horizontal 4-panel, 800×250).

### Why Early Intervention Matters
- Smaller filling = less tooth structure removed = stronger tooth long-term.
- Untreated decay progresses ~1mm per year in enamel, accelerates in dentin.
- Cavity in dentin can reach pulp in 6–12 months if untreated.

## Signs You May Need a Filling

**Content Wireframe:**

Symptom checker:

- **Tooth sensitivity** to sweet, hot, or cold that lingers after stimulus removed
- **Pain when biting** or chewing (pressure on decayed area)
- **Visible hole or dark spot** on the tooth surface
- **Rough edge** or catching when flossing
- **Food impaction** — food consistently getting stuck between same teeth
- **Broken or chipped tooth** exposing inner dentin
- **No symptoms at all** — many cavities are painless until advanced (why X-rays are essential)

- **Callout box:** "**Silent cavities** — you can have a fairly large cavity and feel nothing. The outer enamel has no nerve endings. By the time it hurts, the decay is already deep into dentin or pulp."

## Types of Filling Materials

**Content Wireframe:**

### Composite Resin (Tooth-Colored)
- **Composition:** Glass filler particles in a resin matrix, cured with blue LED light.
- **Pros:** Aesthetic, bonds to tooth structure, preserves more natural tooth, versatile.
- **Cons:** More technique-sensitive, staining over time, may wear faster than amalgam in large restorations.
- **Best for:** Small to medium cavities in all teeth; front teeth aesthetics priority.
- **Lifespan:** 5–10 years (up to 15 with excellent care).

### Amalgam (Silver Fillings)
- **Composition:** Mercury (50%), silver, tin, copper, zinc alloy.
- **Pros:** Extremely durable, wear-resistant, less technique-sensitive, lower cost.
- **Cons:** Dark color expands and contracts with temperature (can crack teeth), mercury controversy (safe per FDA/ADA but patients often prefer alternatives).
- **Best for:** Large cavities in high-stress posterior teeth.
- **Lifespan:** 10–15 years (up to 30+).

### Glass Ionomer
- **Composition:** Silicate glass powder + polyacrylic acid.
- **Pros:** Releases fluoride, bonds chemically to tooth, good for root surfaces.
- **Cons:** Lower strength, less aesthetic, wears faster.
- **Best for:** Root caries, pediatric fillings, temporary restorations, seniors.
- **Lifespan:** 3–5 years.

### Resin Ionomer / Compomer
- **Composition:** Hybrid of composite and glass ionomer.
- **Pros:** Some fluoride release, better aesthetics than GI, easier to use than composite.
- **Cons:** Not as strong as composite or amalgam.
- **Best for:** Non-load-bearing areas, pediatric dentistry.
- **Lifespan:** 3–5 years.

### Gold Fillings (Inlays)
- **Composition:** Gold alloy (cast in a lab).
- **Pros:** Extremely durable, minimal wear to opposing teeth, excellent marginal fit.
- **Cons:** Expensive, requires two visits, visible gold color.
- **Best for:** Patients who want the longest-lasting restoration possible.
- **Lifespan:** 15–30 years.

### Ceramic (Porcelain Inlays/Onlays)
- **Composition:** CAD/CAM milled or pressed ceramic.
- **Pros:** Aesthetic, stain-resistant, strong.
- **Cons:** Expensive, can be brittle if poorly designed, requires two visits (unless CEREC).
- **Best for:** Medium to large cavities where aesthetics matter.
- **Lifespan:** 10–15 years.

## Composite Fillings vs. Amalgam: The Debate

**Content Wireframe:**

| Factor | Composite (Tooth-Colored) | Amalgam (Silver) |
|--------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| **Aesthetics** | Excellent — matches tooth color | Poor — dark silver visible |
| **Tooth preservation** | Bonds to tooth — less removal needed | Requires mechanical retention — more tooth removal |
| **Durability** | 5–10 years | 10–15+ years |
| **Cost** | $150–$450 per tooth | $100–$300 per tooth |
| **Technique sensitivity** | High — moisture contamination causes failure | Low — more forgiving |
| **Mercury content** | None | 50% mercury (bound in alloy, FDA/ADA deemed safe) |
| **Thermal expansion** | Similar to tooth (good) | Different from tooth (can cause cracks) |
| **Setting time** | Sets immediately (light-cured) | Sets over 24 hours |
| **Revision difficulty** | Easy — add more composite | Harder — drill out amalgam |

- Current trend: ~80% of fillings placed in the US are composite; amalgam use declining.
- Our practice: composite-only for anterior and moderate posterior; amalgam reserved for extreme cases.
- **Callout box:** "**Aventura Dental Center** uses exclusively tooth-colored composite restorations for most fillings. We reserve amalgam only for specific high-bite-force scenarios where the patient explicitly prefers it."

## The Filling Procedure Step by Step

**Content Wireframe:**

### Before You Arrive
- No special preparation needed — no fasting, no sedation required for most.
- If anxious, discuss nitrous oxide (laughing gas) or oral sedation.

### Step 1: Local Anesthesia
- Topical gel numbs injection site → slow injection of lidocaine.
- Tooth and surrounding tissue numb within 2–5 minutes.
- **What you feel:** slight pinch, pressure, vibration — no sharp pain.

### Step 2: Isolation
- Rubber dam or cotton rolls isolate the tooth from saliva.
- Saliva contamination is the #1 cause of composite filling failure.

### Step 3: Decay Removal
- High-speed handpiece removes enamel, slow-speed removes soft dentin.
- Explorer confirms all decay removed; cavity cleaned and dried.

### Step 4: Bonding (Composite Only)
- Etchant (35% phosphoric acid) applied for 15 seconds → rinsed → dried.
- Bonding agent (adhesive) brushed on, air-thinned, light-cured.
- Creates a micromechanical lock between tooth and composite.

### Step 5: Layering
- Composite placed in 2mm increments.
- Each layer shaped and light-cured (20 seconds per layer).
- Anatomy built up incrementally — modern composites mimic natural enamel/dentin translucency.

### Step 6: Finishing & Polishing
- Contour with fine diamonds and burs.
- Occlusion adjusted (blue articulating paper).
- Polished to tooth-like luster — final result indistinguishable from natural tooth.

### Step 7: Post-Op Instructions
- Avoid eating until anesthesia wears off (1–3 hours — risk of cheek/tongue bite).
- Mild sensitivity to cold is normal for 48 hours.
- Final set of composite is immediate — you can chew normally after numbness resolves.

## Recovery and Aftercare

**Content Wireframe:**

### First 48 Hours
- Avoid hot beverages until numbness resolves (burn risk).
- Stick to soft foods on the opposite side.
- Sensitivity to cold — normal, resolves within 2–3 days.
- Over-the-counter pain reliever if needed (ibuprofen preferred).

### Long-Term Care
- Brush and floss normally — fillings need the same care as natural teeth.
- Avoid biting hard objects (ice, pens, fingernails) on the filled tooth.
- Nightguard if you grind (bruxism can chip composite fillings).

### When to Call Us
- Persistent sensitivity beyond 2 weeks
- Sharp edge you can feel with your tongue
- Pain on biting that doesn't resolve
- Filling feels rough or chipped
- Food getting stuck around the filling

### Lifespan Expectation
- Composite: 5–10 years (with average wear); can last 15+ with optimal care
- Replace when: margins stain, recurrent decay develops, or filling fractures
- Regular checkups let us monitor fillings for early signs of failure

## Alternatives to Fillings

**Content Wireframe:**

### When a Filling Isn't Enough

| Condition | Alternative | Why |
|-----------|------------|-----|
| Decay too large (>50% of tooth) | Crown | Filling can't protect remaining tooth integrity |
| Decay extends below gumline | Crown or extraction | Filling won't stay dry enough for proper bond |
| Extensive decay with root involvement | Root canal + crown | Pulp is infected; filling addresses only the crown |
| Tooth is non-restorable | Extraction + implant/bridge | Nothing left to fill |

### Remineralization (Pre-Filling)
- Fluoride varnish, MI Paste (casein phosphopeptide), silver diamine fluoride (SDF).
- Can reverse incipient (white spot) lesions — avoiding the drill entirely.
- **Key distinction:** ONLY works on non-cavitated lesions. Once the enamel surface is broken, a filling is needed.
