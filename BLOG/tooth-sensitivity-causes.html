Tooth Sensitivity Causes: When to See a Dentist

Published November 4, 2026 by Aventura Dental Center

AventuraDentalCenter

A cold sip or a sweet bite should not feel like a spark in your tooth. When it does, tooth sensitivity causes usually come back to one problem. The inner layer of the tooth, called dentin, has lost some of its protective cover. The American Dental Association explains that dentin holds microscopic tubes. Heat, cold, acid, or touch can travel through those tubes and reach the nerve. That is why the pain is often sharp and brief, then gone.

This guide is for patients who want to know why a tooth zings, which habits make it worse, and when a short sting needs an exam. It is not a diagnosis. A dentist has to look at the tooth, the gums, and sometimes an X-ray before anyone can say what is going on.

What tooth sensitivity causes feel like

Dentin hypersensitivity is the clinical name for this kind of pain. A 2020 review in BMC Oral Health describes the usual explanation, called the hydrodynamic theory. Fluid inside the open dentin tubules shifts when something cold, sweet, sour, or even a puff of air reaches the tooth. That fluid shift can stimulate the nerve and create a quick jolt.

The feeling is different from a deep toothache that lingers, throbs, or wakes you at night. Sensitivity often:

  • Lasts a second or two, then fades when the trigger is gone.
  • Shows up with ice water, hot coffee, citrus, sweets, or cold air.
  • Hits one tooth, a few teeth, or a whole side of the mouth.
  • Appears while you brush near the gumline.

A lasting ache can still start as sensitivity. Decay, a crack, or an irritated nerve can copy the same first sting. If the pain stays after the cold drink is gone, treat that as a reason to be seen, not as a home experiment.

Common tooth sensitivity causes

The ADA MouthHealthy page on sensitive teeth lists several reasons the protective cover can fail. Enamel covers the crown. A thinner layer called cementum covers the root. Under both is dentin. Lose enamel, lose cementum, or lose gum tissue over the root, and the tubes are open.

These are the causes patients ask about most often. More than one can be true at the same time.

Worn enamel

Enamel does not grow back. Acid from foods and drinks, stomach acid, and years of grinding can thin it. Once dentin shows through, cold and sweet foods reach the nerve more easily. Whitening products can also irritate teeth that already have thin enamel. A whiter shade is not worth chasing if every cold drink hurts. Ask before you start another round of strips or gel.

Gum recession and exposed roots

Roots are not built like crowns. Cementum is thin, and brushing with a hard hand can wear gum tissue back over time. Gum disease can do the same by loosening the tissue that should cover the root. The tooth may look longer. The tender band is usually right at the gumline, not deep in the jaw.

Decay, cracks, and old dental work

A cavity, a fractured tooth, or a filling that no longer seals can open a path to dentin. Recent dental work can also leave a tooth tender for a short time while the nerve settles. Tenderness that fades over a few days is common after a filling or cleaning. Pain that builds, throbs, or wakes you is a different story and needs a look.

If you already know a tooth has a crack or a failing filling, the guide to tooth pain covers how dentists sort lingering pain from a short sensitive sting. This page stays with the cause question.

Everyday triggers that make a sensitive tooth sting

A trigger is not always the cause. Ice water does not create the open tubes. It only sets off a tooth that is already unprotected. Patients in Aventura often notice the same short list:

  • Cold water, ice, and chilled fruit.
  • Hot coffee or tea, especially after a cold drink.
  • Citrus, soda, sports drinks, wine, and sour candy.
  • Sugar touching a worn spot or the edge of a filling.
  • Cold air on a front tooth while you walk outside.
  • Toothbrush bristles or floss at a receded gumline.

Acid is a special problem because it softens enamel. Brushing hard right after a soda or a citrus snack can scrub away more tooth than the drink alone. Rinsing with plain water and waiting a bit before you brush is a safer habit. It does not fix a cavity or a crack.

Sudden sensitivity in one tooth still deserves attention even when you cannot see a cavity. A hairline crack, a new groove from grinding, or a root just starting to show can all sting before a dark spot appears. Two front teeth that hurt together often point to brushing habits, acid, or gum recession rather than one deep cavity, but only an exam can tell.

What you can safely try at home

Home steps can calm a mild sting. They cannot tell you why the tooth is open. Use them as a bridge to an exam, not as a substitute, if the pain keeps returning.

  1. Switch to a soft toothbrush and use small circles along the gumline. Scrubbing harder does not clean better, and it can worsen recession.
  2. Use a fluoride toothpaste made for sensitive teeth. The ADA notes that these pastes help block sensation, and they often need repeated use before the sting eases.
  3. Limit long sips of soda, citrus, and other acids. Drink them with a meal when you can, then rinse with water.
  4. Skip whitening strips and abrasive whitening scrubs while a tooth is zinging.
  5. If you wake with a sore jaw, mention grinding at your visit. A night guard is a dentist's call, not a guess from a store shelf.
  6. Do not place aspirin, alcohol, or essential oils on the gum. That can burn tissue and hide the real problem.

Call rather than wait if swelling, fever, a bad taste, or pain that lingers after the trigger is gone shows up. Those signs are not simple sensitivity. Warning signs that need a dental visit

A single zing from ice cream can wait for a regular checkup if it fades at once and does not return. Book sooner when any of these show up:

  • Pain that lasts more than a few seconds after the hot or cold is gone.
  • Pain on biting or releasing a bite.
  • A cracked, chipped, or dark spot you can see.
  • Gums that bleed, look puffy, or have pulled away from the tooth.
  • Sensitivity that spreads or gets worse over a week or two.
  • Swelling, fever, or a bad taste, which can mean infection.

Sensitivity with no obvious cavity is still a reason to be examined. Enamel wear and root exposure do not always leave a hole you can see in the mirror. Waiting until the sting becomes a toothache can turn a small repair into a larger one. For many families, a dental checkup is the right first step. If the tooth is already throbbing or the gum is swollen, call the emergency dentist line and describe the symptoms so the office can tell you how quickly to come in.

How a dentist finds the cause

Finding the cause is a process of ruling things out. At Aventura Dental Center, a visit starts with your history and the story of the pain. The dentist asks which tooth hurts, which foods set it off, and how long the sting lasts. They also ask if you grind, sip acids, or recently had dental work. Then they look at the teeth, the gumline, old fillings, and signs of wear.

A short test often tells a lot. Air, cold, or a gentle touch on one spot may reproduce the zing and show whether the tender area is the root, a crack line, or the edge of a filling. X-rays are taken when they are needed to look for decay between teeth or a problem at the root, not as a routine extra. The nerve can also be checked when the pain lingers, because a dying nerve and open dentin do not get the same care.

What happens next depends on what the exam shows. None of these is a promise for every tooth. Healing and the right fix depend on the exam.

  • Open root surfaces may improve with a desensitizing paste, a fluoride treatment, or a thin bonded layer over the spot.
  • A cavity or a broken filling edge may need a tooth-colored filling once the decay is removed.
  • A cracked or heavily worn tooth may need a crown if a filling will not hold the tooth together.
  • A nerve that stays inflamed may need root canal treatment. That step is for a tooth that cannot settle with simpler care, not the first move for a mild cold sting.
  • Gum loss that leaves a wide root exposed is sometimes treated with a gum graft. Whether that fits you is a clinical decision after the exam.

The dentist should be able to point to the likely cause in plain language before you choose a next step. If the cause is a habit, such as scrubbing the gumline or all-day soda sipping, changing that habit matters as much as any material placed on the tooth.

Frequently asked questions

What causes tooth sensitivity?

Most tooth sensitivity causes trace back to exposed dentin. Enamel wear, gum recession, decay, cracks, worn fillings, and gum disease can all uncover it. Acid, hard brushing, and grinding are common reasons the cover thins in the first place.

Why are my teeth sensitive all of a sudden?

A new sting can follow a recent cleaning, filling, whitening session, a new crack, or a bout of acidic drinks. It can also be the first time you notice a root that has been slowly uncovering. If it does not fade in a few days, or if it is getting worse, have that tooth examined.

Can you have tooth sensitivity without a cavity?

Yes. Gum recession, worn enamel, and grinding can hurt when there is no hole in the tooth. A dentist still needs to rule out a hidden cavity or crack, because those do not always show up in a bathroom mirror.

Why is my tooth sensitive to cold but there is no cavity?

Cold is a classic trigger when dentin tubes are open. The root surface and thinned enamel respond fast to temperature. No visible cavity does not rule out a small crack or early decay between teeth, which an exam and X-ray can check.

When should I see a dentist about tooth sensitivity?

See a dentist if the sting lasts after the trigger is gone, pain hits when you bite, the gum bleeds or recedes, or the problem is new and sticking around. Swelling, fever, or a bad taste should be called in promptly. For a routine concern, request a checkup. For strong pain, call and ask how soon you should be seen.
