Tooth With Abscess: Signs, Causes, and Treatment

Published November 1, 2026 by Aventura Dental Center

AventuraDentalCenter

A swollen gum or persistent toothache can leave you wondering whether the problem will pass on its own. If a tooth with abscess is the cause, waiting may allow the infection to affect nearby tissues. The signs aren’t always dramatic: pain may come and go, or a small bump may be the first clue. A dental exam can help identify where the infection began and what treatment may be needed. This guide covers common causes and symptoms, explains how dentists address an abscess, and shares practical steps to take while arranging care. If you have trouble breathing or swallowing, seek emergency care right away.

Key Takeaways

  • A suspected abscess needs a dental evaluation: Pain can fade or an abscess can drain while infection remains, so contact a dentist rather than waiting for symptoms to pass.
  • Match your response to your symptoms: Seek dental care promptly for tooth pain, swelling, pus, or fever. Get emergency medical care for trouble breathing or swallowing, or rapidly worsening swelling.
  • Protect your teeth with routine care: Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste, floss daily, and keep regular dental visits. Have cavities, gum problems, and tooth damage checked early.

What Is a Tooth Abscess?

A tooth abscess is a pocket of pus caused by a bacterial infection in or around a tooth. It may cause pain, swelling, and tenderness, though symptoms can vary. Where the infection starts helps a dentist determine what care may be needed.

How bacteria cause an infection

Bacteria can cause an abscess when they get past the tooth’s outer layers and reach the soft tissue inside, or infect the gums and supporting tissues. A cavity, crack, or injury can create an opening for bacteria to enter. The body responds to the infection, and pus may collect in a pocket around the affected area.

An abscess needs professional dental care. It will not reliably clear up on its own, and the infection can damage the tooth or spread to nearby tissues. The Cleveland Clinic’s overview of tooth abscesses explains how these infections develop. If you have ongoing tooth pain or swelling, contact a dentist for an evaluation.

Periapical vs. periodontal abscesses

Dentists classify an abscess by where it begins. A periapical abscess forms at the tip of a tooth’s root. It can develop when decay or a crack lets bacteria reach the pulp, the soft tissue inside the tooth. A periodontal abscess starts in the gums and tissues that support the tooth, and may be linked to gum disease.

A gingival abscess is another type, forming in the gum tissue itself. These infections can have similar symptoms, so the source may not be clear without an examination. A dentist may also take X-rays to assess the tooth and surrounding bone. The Cleveland Clinic describes the main types of tooth abscesses, which require care suited to the location and cause of the infection.

Why children and adults can develop abscesses

People of any age can develop a tooth abscess. In children, untreated cavities may allow bacteria to reach the inner part of a tooth. Consistent brushing with fluoride toothpaste, help with brushing when needed, and regular dental visits can help catch decay early. A guide to dental abscesses notes that children with poor dental hygiene are at increased risk of periapical abscesses.

Adults may develop an abscess after untreated decay, a cracked tooth, or infection in the gums. Having had dental work does not rule out a later infection. If you or your child has tooth pain, swelling, or a pimple-like bump on the gums, arrange a dental assessment. What Causes a Tooth Abscess?

A tooth abscess forms when bacteria infect the inside of a tooth or the tissues around it. The infection can create a pocket of pus near the root or along the gumline. Several dental problems can give bacteria a way in, and an abscess may develop even if the tooth does not hurt at first.

Untreated tooth decay

A cavity starts when acids produced by mouth bacteria wear away a tooth’s protective outer layer. If decay continues, it can reach the pulp, the softer inner part of the tooth that contains nerves and blood vessels. Bacteria may infect the pulp and travel toward the root, where an abscess can form.

Early decay may not cause noticeable pain, so a tooth can need treatment before you realize there is a problem. Regular dental exams help identify cavities while they are smaller and easier to treat. The Cleveland Clinic explains how bacteria entering a tooth can cause an abscess. If you notice tooth pain, sensitivity, or a visible dark spot, contact a dentist for an evaluation.

Cracked, damaged, or injured teeth

A crack, chip, or dental injury can create an opening that lets bacteria reach the inner tooth. Damage may happen after a fall or sports injury, from biting something hard, or through long-term wear. Even a small crack may be hard to spot, and symptoms can take time to appear.

If bacteria reach the pulp through damaged enamel, an infection may develop and spread toward the root. A dentist can assess the tooth and recommend treatment based on the type and extent of the damage. That may include a filling or crown to protect the tooth, or other care if the inner tissue is infected. If you injure a tooth or notice a crack, arrange a dental visit promptly rather than waiting for pain to worsen.

Gum disease and infection around a tooth

An abscess can form in the gums as well as inside a tooth. Gum disease can damage the tissues that support teeth and create pockets where bacteria collect. If bacteria infect one of these areas, pus may build up and cause a periodontal abscess. You may notice swollen or tender gums, drainage, or a bad taste.

Bleeding when you brush or floss can be a sign of gum inflammation, even if you do not have significant pain. A dentist can check the gums and determine whether infection or another issue needs treatment. Gum disease and tooth injuries can contribute to dental abscesses, so do not ignore changes around a tooth or along the gumline.

How poor oral hygiene and delayed care increase risk

Plaque is a sticky layer of bacteria that forms on teeth. Brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and flossing daily help remove plaque before it contributes to cavities or gum disease. When these habits are inconsistent, bacteria can build up and increase the risk of problems that may lead to an abscess.

Delaying care can also give decay, a cracked tooth, or gum infection more time to worsen. A small cavity or minor injury may not seem urgent, but an examination can help identify what is happening and what care is needed. Keep regular dental appointments, and contact a dentist if you notice pain, swelling, pus, or damage to a tooth. You can contact Aventura Dental Center about an evaluation and treatment options.

How Can You Recognize a Tooth Abscess?

A tooth abscess is a pocket of infection that can develop around a tooth or in the nearby gum tissue. Symptoms often include pain, swelling, or sensitivity, but they can vary from person to person. You might have a strong toothache, notice a small bump on your gum, or taste something unpleasant. In some cases, symptoms are mild or change over time.

Pay attention to new or worsening symptoms, especially when they appear together. For example, tooth pain with gum swelling, facial swelling, fever, or pus should be assessed promptly by a dentist. These signs can point to an infection, though other dental problems may cause similar symptoms. A dental exam, and sometimes an X-ray, can help identify what is causing the discomfort.

Don’t wait for pain to become severe before seeking advice. An abscess generally needs professional dental treatment, and a change in pain does not necessarily mean the infection has gone away. Contact a dentist if you suspect an abscess, even if symptoms seem to settle. If swelling is spreading quickly or you have trouble breathing or swallowing, seek emergency care right away.

Persistent, throbbing pain or pain when chewing

A persistent, throbbing toothache is a common sign of an abscess. The pain may spread toward your jaw, ear, or neck, and the tooth may feel tender when you bite down or touch it. Chewing can make the discomfort more noticeable. Some people also experience sensitivity to hot or cold foods and drinks.

The pain may come and go, or it may make eating and sleeping difficult. It can also be hard to tell which tooth is causing the problem if the ache spreads. The Cleveland Clinic’s overview of tooth abscess symptoms includes severe, throbbing pain and pain when chewing. If tooth pain persists or worsens, contact a dentist for an evaluation instead of waiting for it to pass.

Tooth sensitivity, swollen gums, facial swelling, or a pimple-like bump

An abscess may make the gum near a tooth red, swollen, or sore. You might see a small, pimple-like bump on the gum, or notice swelling in your cheek or face. The affected tooth may also feel sensitive to pressure, hot or cold temperatures, or touch.

These signs can start subtly, but they still deserve attention. Don’t squeeze or poke a bump, since that won’t treat the cause and may irritate the area. Contact a dentist promptly if you notice swelling around a tooth, particularly if it is getting worse. A dental exam can help identify the source of the swelling, and X-rays may be used to check for problems beneath the surface. If facial swelling is rapidly increasing or spreading, seek urgent care.

Pus, a bad taste, fever, or feeling unwell

An abscess can release pus through a small opening in the gum. You may notice fluid near the tooth, bad breath, or a bitter or unpleasant taste in your mouth. Fever, swollen glands, or feeling generally unwell can also occur. Not everyone experiences all of these symptoms, and their presence does not confirm an abscess on its own.

The MedlinePlus guide to tooth abscesses lists symptoms such as swollen gums, a pimple-like bump, fever, bad breath, and a bitter taste. Contact a dentist promptly if you notice pus, swelling, or fever. Trouble breathing or swallowing, or rapidly worsening swelling, requires emergency care.

Why pain may ease or symptoms may stay subtle while infection remains

Pain can sometimes ease even though an infection is still present. If the nerve inside the tooth becomes damaged, it may stop sending pain signals as usual. An abscess may also drain, reducing pressure for a while. Neither change means the infection has healed, so don’t use pain level alone to decide whether you need care.

Symptoms can also be mild, intermittent, or difficult to pinpoint. If you noticed a bump, swelling, or unusual taste and it later went away, contact a dentist rather than assuming the problem has resolved. A dentist can examine the area and explain the next steps. Why Should You Treat a Tooth Abscess Promptly?

A tooth abscess won’t usually resolve without dental treatment. Even if symptoms seem mild or improve, the infection may still be active. Contact a dentist promptly if you notice tooth pain, swelling, pus, or a fever. An examination can help identify the infection and determine the right next step.

How infection can spread to nearby tissues, the jaw, or elsewhere

An abscess is a pocket of infection near a tooth. Without treatment, bacteria can spread into nearby gums, the jawbone, or the soft tissues of the face and neck. In some cases, the infection can enter the bloodstream and affect other areas of the body. How quickly an infection changes can vary, so it’s not possible to judge its severity by pain alone. A dentist can examine the area and recommend care based on what they find. The Cleveland Clinic’s guide to tooth abscesses explains why timely treatment is important.

Why a burst abscess or reduced pain does not mean it has healed

An abscess may drain on its own, releasing pus and temporarily easing pressure. Pain can also lessen if the tissue inside the tooth has become severely damaged. Neither change confirms that the infection is gone. Bacteria may remain around the tooth and continue to affect nearby tissues, even if the area feels better for a while. Don’t use a burst bump or reduced pain as a reason to put off care. Contact a dentist for an evaluation, and describe any changes in pain, swelling, or drainage.

Rare but serious complications of an untreated abscess

Serious complications are uncommon, but an untreated abscess can sometimes spread to the jawbone or tissues in the face and neck. In rare cases, the infection can travel farther and cause severe illness, including infections that affect the heart or brain. These outcomes are not typical, but they underline why a suspected abscess needs professional attention. Seek emergency medical care if you have trouble breathing or swallowing, or if swelling worsens rapidly. For tooth pain, pus, fever, or localized swelling, contact a dentist promptly. The Cleveland Clinic also outlines potential complications and symptoms that need urgent attention.

How timely care can relieve pain and protect your oral health

A dentist can treat the source of the infection, help relieve pressure and discomfort, and assess whether the tooth can be saved. Depending on the cause and extent of the infection, treatment may include draining the abscess, a root canal treatment, or removing the tooth. How Do Dentists Treat a Tooth Abscess?

A dentist treats a tooth abscess by addressing the infection and its source. The right approach depends on which tooth is affected, how much damage has occurred, and whether the infection has spread. Your dentist will examine your mouth and may take X-rays to locate the problem and assess the tooth. Treatment may involve draining the abscess, performing a root canal to preserve the tooth, or removing the tooth if it cannot be saved.

Antibiotics may be part of care in some situations, but they do not eliminate the source of infection inside a tooth. Professional dental treatment is still needed, even if pain eases or the abscess drains on its own. While waiting for an appointment, gentle rinsing or a cool compress may help with discomfort, but these measures are temporary. Don’t try to squeeze or puncture the swollen area.

If you have tooth pain, swelling, pus, or a fever, contact a dentist promptly. The team at Aventura Dental Center can assess your symptoms and explain potential treatment options.

Use an exam and X-rays to locate the infection

Your dentist will ask about your symptoms and examine your teeth, gums, and the inside of your mouth. They may gently tap on a tooth or ask you to bite down, since pressure can make an infected tooth hurt. They’ll also check for swelling, changes in the gums, or a small pimple-like bump that may show where the infection is draining.

Dental X-rays can help identify the affected tooth and show changes around its root or the nearby bone. This matters because the source of pain is not always obvious from the symptoms alone. The exam and imaging help your dentist decide which treatment is appropriate. MedlinePlus describes how dentists use an exam and X-rays to identify a tooth abscess.

Drain the abscess and use root canal treatment to save the tooth

A dentist may drain an abscess to release pus and relieve pressure. Treatment must also address what caused the infection. If bacteria have reached the soft tissue inside the tooth, a root canal may help save it. During the procedure, the dentist removes the infected tissue, cleans the inside of the tooth, and seals it. A crown may be recommended afterward to protect the treated tooth.

The best approach depends on the tooth’s condition and how far the infection has progressed. Ask your dentist what the procedure will involve, what to expect during recovery, and whether you’ll need a follow-up restoration. MedlinePlus explains that root canal treatment may be used to try to save an abscessed tooth.

Know when you may need an extraction

If an abscessed tooth is too damaged to repair, your dentist may recommend removing it. An extraction removes the tooth that is contributing to the infection, and the dentist may drain the abscess as part of treatment. Whether this is needed depends on the tooth’s condition, the location of the infection, and your overall oral health.

It’s reasonable to ask why extraction is being recommended and whether a root canal could be an option. If the tooth does need to come out, your dentist can discuss how the space may affect chewing and whether a replacement tooth should be considered later. Cleveland Clinic describes extraction and drainage as treatment options when an abscessed tooth cannot be saved.

Understand when antibiotics help, and why they cannot replace dental treatment

A dentist may prescribe antibiotics when an infection is spreading or affecting tissues beyond the tooth. However, antibiotics alone usually cannot remove the source of a dental abscess. The tooth still needs appropriate treatment, such as drainage, a root canal, or extraction. Without that care, the underlying problem may remain even if symptoms temporarily improve.

If antibiotics are prescribed, follow your dentist’s directions and let them know about allergies, other medicines, and health conditions. Don’t take leftover antibiotics or someone else’s prescription. Contact your dentist if symptoms worsen or fail to improve as expected. Cleveland Clinic explains that antibiotics may fight bacteria but do not remove the source of infection in the tooth.

Manage discomfort safely while arranging care

Contact a dentist as soon as you notice possible signs of an abscess. While you’re arranging care, gently rinsing with warm salt water and spitting it out may provide temporary comfort. A cool compress held against the outside of your cheek may also help with discomfort or swelling. These steps can ease symptoms, but they won’t clear the infection.

If you’re considering an over-the-counter pain reliever, follow the package directions. Check with a dentist, doctor, or pharmacist if you have a health condition, take other medication, are pregnant, or aren’t sure what’s safe for you. Don’t put aspirin directly on your tooth or gum, as it may irritate the tissue. MedlinePlus lists rinses and pain relievers as measures that may help ease symptoms.

Know why home remedies cannot cure an abscess, and what to avoid

Rinses and cold compresses may make you feel more comfortable for a short time, but they can’t remove the infection inside a tooth or treat its cause. An abscess may also drain on its own, but that doesn’t mean the infection has cleared. Keep your dental appointment even if the pain or swelling seems to ease.

Don’t squeeze, puncture, or try to drain a swollen area yourself. Avoid putting harsh substances or unapproved remedies on your gums, since they may irritate or injure the tissue. Contact a dentist for advice on what to do while you wait for treatment. Home care may ease symptoms, but it cannot eliminate a tooth abscess.

Follow up and clarify your treatment instructions

After treatment, follow the instructions your dentist provides. They may cover eating, cleaning the treated area, taking prescribed medication, and scheduling a follow-up visit. If you’re unsure about a direction, call the dental office to check. Contact your dentist if you develop new or worsening symptoms, and don’t change a prescribed medication without speaking with your dentist or prescriber.

A tooth treated with a root canal may need a crown or another restoration for protection. Keep recommended appointments so your dentist can check healing and complete any planned care. With timely treatment and appropriate follow-up, many people recover well, and a treated tooth may remain functional for years. When Should You Seek Emergency Care for a Tooth Abscess?

A suspected tooth abscess needs prompt attention, even if the pain feels manageable. The symptoms you have can help determine where to seek care: serious breathing or swallowing problems call for emergency medical attention, while tooth pain, gum swelling, fever, or pus mean you should contact a dentist promptly. A dental evaluation can identify the source of the infection and help prevent it from worsening.

Get emergency care for trouble breathing or swallowing, or rapidly worsening swelling

Go to the emergency room immediately if you have trouble breathing or swallowing, or severe swelling in your face or neck that is getting worse quickly. These symptoms may signal that an infection is affecting nearby areas and could interfere with your airway. A high fever, confusion, or extreme dizziness with a suspected abscess also warrants urgent medical attention.

Don’t wait for a dental appointment if these symptoms develop. Call 911 if you cannot get to the emergency room safely or your breathing is seriously affected. The Cleveland Clinic’s guidance on tooth abscesses explains which symptoms need immediate medical care. After emergency evaluation, follow the care team’s instructions and arrange dental treatment to address the source of the infection.

Contact a dentist promptly for tooth pain, swelling, fever, or pus

Call a dentist as soon as possible if you have persistent or throbbing tooth pain, pain when chewing, swollen gums, a pimple-like bump, pus, a bad taste, or a fever. These symptoms can be signs of an abscess. Even if pain eases or the abscess drains, the infection may remain and still needs professional care.

How Can You Prevent a Tooth Abscess?

Many tooth abscesses begin with problems such as untreated decay, a damaged tooth, or gum disease. A consistent home-care routine and regular dental visits can help catch these concerns early and lower the chance of infection.

Brush with fluoride toothpaste, floss daily, and keep regular dental visits

Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and clean between your teeth daily with floss or another tool recommended by your dentist. These habits help remove plaque, which can contribute to tooth decay and gum disease. Limiting sugary foods and drinks can also help protect your teeth. If you play contact sports, wear a properly fitted mouthguard to reduce the risk of dental injuries.

Regular checkups give your dentist a chance to look for early signs of decay, gum problems, or damage, even when you don’t feel pain. Treat cavities, damaged teeth, and gum disease early

Contact a dentist if you notice tooth sensitivity, a chip or crack, a damaged filling, or swollen or bleeding gums. A cavity can deepen over time, and a crack may allow bacteria to reach the inner part of the tooth. Gum disease can affect the tissues around teeth. Early evaluation helps your dentist identify the problem and explain appropriate treatment options.

Don’t wait for severe pain before seeking advice. Some dental problems cause mild or occasional symptoms at first, but still need attention. Early treatment can help address dental problems before they worsen. If you’re unsure whether a symptom needs care, call your dental office and describe what you’re experiencing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a tooth abscess go away on its own? No. Pain may ease or pus may drain, but the infection can remain. Arrange a dental evaluation even if symptoms improve.

What should I do if I think I have a tooth abscess? Call a dentist promptly and describe your symptoms, including when they began and whether you have swelling, fever, or drainage. A dental exam and possibly X-rays can help determine the cause and appropriate treatment.

Will I need a root canal or an extraction? It depends on the tooth’s condition and where the infection started. A root canal may help preserve a tooth, while an extraction may be recommended if the tooth is too damaged to repair. Your dentist can explain the options after examining you.

Can antibiotics cure a tooth abscess? Antibiotics may be prescribed in some cases, especially if infection is spreading, but they usually cannot remove the source inside the tooth. Dental treatment is still needed.
