Composite Fillings vs Amalgam Fillings: Which to Choose?

Published November 1, 2026 by Aventura Dental Center

AventuraDentalCenter

A cavity may be small, but choosing how to repair it can raise plenty of questions. Should you choose a tooth-colored filling that blends in, or a silver-colored option with a long history in dentistry? When comparing composite fillings vs amalgam fillings, appearance is only one part of the decision. The cavity’s size and location, the tooth’s condition, your bite, and your dental coverage can all matter. Each material has advantages and limitations, and one may suit your needs better than the other. Here’s what to know about the differences, what to ask your dentist, and how the team at Aventura Dental Center can help you make an informed choice.

Key Takeaways

  • Let the tooth’s needs guide the choice: Cavity size, location, bite pressure, and remaining healthy tooth structure all affect which filling may work best.
  • Weigh appearance, durability, and cost: Composite is tooth-colored and bonds to the tooth, while amalgam is silver-colored and may suit some back teeth. Costs and insurance coverage vary.
  • Discuss your options with your dentist: Share health concerns and preferences, ask about expected lifespan and costs, and have existing fillings checked before considering replacement.

What Are Composite and Amalgam Fillings?

Fillings repair teeth affected by decay by replacing damaged tooth structure. Composite resin and dental amalgam are two materials dentists may use, but they look and behave differently. The right option depends on the tooth and cavity, as well as your preferences. Your dentist can explain which materials are suitable for your treatment.

Composite: tooth-colored resin with glass or ceramic

Composite fillings combine acrylic resin with finely ground glass or ceramic particles. A dentist can match the material to your natural tooth shade, making it a popular choice for front teeth and other visible areas. Composite bonds to the tooth, and the dentist shapes and hardens it in layers to restore the damaged area.

The appearance and bonding properties can make composite appealing, but suitability depends on the cavity and the tooth’s condition. The time needed to place it may also vary with the size and location of the repair. Read more about composite filling materials, then ask your dentist how the material may perform in your case.

Amalgam: metal alloy that includes mercury

Dental amalgam is a blend of metals that includes silver, tin, copper, and mercury. It has a silver color, which can become darker over time and is typically more noticeable than composite. Amalgam has a long history of use in dentistry, and its strength can make it a suitable option for some teeth that handle substantial chewing pressure.

Like any filling material, amalgam has features to weigh against your needs and preferences. If you have questions about its ingredients, appearance, or use in a particular tooth, raise them with your dentist before treatment. This overview of amalgam filling materials provides more background.

How cavity size, location, and tooth condition shape your options

The choice isn’t based on material alone. A small cavity in a visible area may make composite worth considering for its tooth-colored finish. For a back tooth, your dentist may focus more on chewing pressure, cavity size, and how much healthy tooth structure remains. Your bite and habits, such as clenching or grinding, can also affect the recommendation.

After examining the tooth, your dentist can explain which materials are appropriate and what trade-offs to consider. At Aventura Dental Center, you can discuss how the cavity’s location and condition shape your options, along with any questions you have about the procedure.

How Do Appearance and Tooth Preservation Compare?

Composite and amalgam fillings differ in appearance and in how they attach to a tooth. The right choice depends on more than visibility: your dentist will also consider the cavity’s size and location, the tooth’s condition, and how much healthy structure remains.

Composite’s tooth color versus amalgam’s silver color

Composite fillings are made from tooth-colored resin and can be matched to the shade of your natural teeth. This makes them a discreet option for cavities on front teeth or other visible areas. The match may not be exact, but a composite filling is generally less noticeable than a metal one.

Amalgam fillings have a silver appearance, so they may stand out when you smile or speak, especially in visible areas. Some people prefer a less visible restoration, while others may be comfortable with the look of amalgam on a back tooth. Your preference matters, but so do the tooth’s needs. You can compare the appearance of composite and amalgam fillings and ask your dentist which materials are suitable for your cavity.

How bonding can reduce cavity preparation

Composite bonds to the tooth, which can help support the remaining structure and may allow the dentist to preserve more healthy tissue during preparation. Amalgam is held in place by the shape of the prepared cavity, so placement may require a different approach. The amount of tooth structure removed depends on the filling’s size and location, along with the condition of the tooth.

Bonding does not mean that no healthy tissue will be removed. Your dentist still needs to clear away decay and prepare the area so the filling can be placed securely. Composite also requires careful placement in a clean, dry area. Read more about how composite bonding can affect cavity preparation, then ask what the process would involve for your tooth.

How tooth damage affects healthy tooth removal

The extent of the damage helps determine how much tooth structure needs treatment. A small cavity may leave plenty of healthy tissue around it. A larger cavity, an old filling, or a crack may mean more of the tooth is weakened. Your dentist removes decay and damaged tissue, then considers which restoration can repair the area and protect what remains.

The amount of sound tooth structure left can affect whether a filling is suitable, as well as which material your dentist recommends. Ask how much tissue needs to be removed and why, and whether another restoration may be appropriate. How Do Durability and Lifespan Compare?

How filling size and chewing pressure affect performance

A filling’s durability depends in part on how much of the tooth it repairs and where it sits. Back teeth, especially molars, handle substantial chewing pressure, while front teeth usually face less force. Amalgam is known for its strength and may be considered for teeth that do heavy chewing. Composite fillings match the tooth’s color, so they’re often chosen for visible areas. The best option depends on the cavity’s size, location, and the amount of healthy tooth remaining. Lighthouse Dental’s comparison outlines these differences, but your dentist can assess how they apply to your tooth.

How wear and damage may lead to repair or replacement

Neither type of filling lasts forever. With time, a filling may wear down, crack, loosen, or develop a gap where the filling meets the tooth. These changes can allow bacteria to collect and may lead to new decay. Amalgam fillings often last 10 to 15 years or longer, while composite fillings may last up to about 10 years with proper care. These are general estimates, not guarantees. Your oral health, the filling’s size and location, and everyday habits all affect its lifespan. Regular exams give your dentist a chance to check existing fillings and recommend repair or replacement if needed, as Tend Dental explains.

How placement, oral habits, and checkups affect fillings

Placement and daily habits can also affect how well a filling holds up. Composite is applied in layers and hardened with a special light, so placing it may take longer. Because it bonds to the tooth, composite may require removing less healthy tooth structure than amalgam, depending on the cavity. After treatment, avoid habits such as chewing ice or biting hard objects, which can put extra stress on fillings. If you grind your teeth, ask your dentist whether a night guard may help protect them. Brush and floss regularly, and keep up with checkups so your dentist can spot changes early. What Do Composite and Amalgam Fillings Cost?

Filling costs depend on the material, the size and location of the cavity, your insurance coverage, and whether the tooth needs additional care. The prices below are general estimates, not a quote for your treatment. Your dentist can assess the tooth and explain the expected cost before starting.

Why composite may cost more

Composite fillings are made from tooth-colored resin. The dentist applies the material in layers, shapes it to the tooth, and hardens it with a special light. This process may take more time than placing an amalgam filling, which can contribute to the difference in price.

As a general estimate, composite fillings may cost about $150 to $300 per tooth, while amalgam fillings may range from $50 to $150 per tooth. Actual prices vary by practice, location, and treatment needs. A comparison of filling materials and costs offers similar ranges, but your dentist’s exam is the best way to get an estimate tailored to your tooth.

How tooth location, filling size, and insurance affect your costs

A small cavity may cost less to fill than a larger one, since it may require less material and time. Location matters, too. Back teeth handle more chewing pressure, and a filling that covers several surfaces may require additional work. Your dentist will consider the cavity’s size, the tooth’s condition, and which material is suitable.

Many dental plans help cover fillings, but coverage can differ by material and tooth location. Some plans may pay for a composite filling on a back tooth only up to the amount they would cover for amalgam. Check your benefits and ask your dental office to verify coverage. Other factors that affect filling costs include the amount of tooth damage and any care needed beyond the filling.

What to ask about your treatment estimate

Ask for a written estimate that identifies the tooth, recommended material, and any additional services or fees. Check whether your insurer has reviewed the proposed treatment, and ask if you may owe the difference when the plan covers one material at a lower rate.

It can also help to ask why your dentist recommends a particular filling, whether another option could work, and what might change the estimate. If the tooth needs care beyond a filling, ask what that treatment involves and how it affects the total cost. Are Composite and Amalgam Fillings Safe?

Mercury in amalgam and materials in composite

Dental amalgam contains elemental mercury combined with metals such as silver, tin, and copper. The FDA’s guidance on dental amalgam says amalgam is safe for most people, while advising some patients to consider other materials when appropriate. Composite fillings are made from resin and glass or ceramic particles, and they do not contain mercury. Both materials are widely used in dentistry, but the right choice depends on the tooth, the size of the cavity, and your health history. Ask your dentist to explain the materials available and why they recommend a particular filling for you.

When to discuss pregnancy, a child’s filling, or health concerns

Tell your dentist if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, choosing a filling for a child under six, or managing a condition that could affect your treatment options. The FDA advises these groups, along with people with certain neurological or kidney conditions, to discuss alternatives to new amalgam fillings when appropriate. This guidance does not mean you should put off needed dental care. Share your medical history and any concerns before treatment so your dentist can weigh the benefits and risks of each material. Sensitivities and uncommon material allergies

Allergic reactions to filling materials are uncommon, but it’s important to mention any known allergies or previous reactions to dental products. Composite resin and amalgam contain different ingredients, so your dentist can review the materials and help determine what may be suitable for you. After a filling, contact your dentist if you notice persistent irritation, swelling, or other symptoms that concern you. These symptoms can have several causes and don’t necessarily mean you’re allergic to the filling. Sharing when they began and how they feel can help your dentist assess the issue and recommend next steps.

Why dentists usually leave intact amalgam fillings in place

If an amalgam filling is intact and the tooth is healthy, a dentist usually won’t recommend removing it solely because it contains mercury or looks silver. Removing a filling can release a temporary increase in mercury vapor and may require taking away healthy tooth structure. The FDA advises against removing sound amalgam fillings unless there is a medical need, such as a confirmed allergy. A dentist may recommend repair or replacement if the filling is damaged, decay has formed beneath it, or it no longer protects the tooth. If you’re concerned, ask your dentist to check the filling and explain whether monitoring, repair, or replacement is appropriate.

How Do You Choose the Right Filling?

Weigh appearance, tooth preservation, durability, and cost

No single filling material is right for every tooth. Composite fillings are matched to your tooth color, so they’re often preferred for visible areas. The material bonds to the tooth and may allow the dentist to preserve more healthy structure during preparation. Amalgam fillings have a silver appearance and can be a durable option for some teeth.

Cost, expected lifespan, and the size of the cavity also matter. Your dental coverage may affect your out-of-pocket cost, so ask for an estimate before treatment. Filling longevity varies based on factors such as the tooth’s location and the restoration’s size. This comparison of composite and amalgam fillings can help you prepare questions about the trade-offs.

Consider tooth location, chewing needs, and your preferences

A filling in a visible front tooth has different demands from one in a back molar. Molars handle substantial chewing pressure, so your dentist will consider the tooth’s location, your bite, and how much of the tooth needs repair. Composite is often chosen for its natural appearance, while amalgam may be considered for some back teeth.

The cavity’s size and the amount of remaining healthy tooth also shape your options. If damage is extensive, your dentist may discuss a crown or another restoration instead of a filling. Consider what matters most to you, such as appearance, preserving tooth structure, and expected maintenance. Ask about risks, alternatives, lifespan, and future repairs

Before treatment, ask why your dentist recommends a particular material and whether another option could work for your tooth. Find out how long the filling may last, what signs of wear or damage to watch for, and whether a small chip can be repaired. Lifespan depends on the filling’s size and location, your bite, and habits such as grinding your teeth.

Mention any health concerns or material sensitivities, and ask about the benefits and limitations of each option. If you’re considering treatment for a child, bring that up as well. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I choose my filling material? Your preferences matter, but the best choice also depends on the cavity’s size and location, your bite, and how much healthy tooth remains. Ask your dentist which options are suitable and why.

Are composite fillings always the better choice for visible teeth? Their tooth-colored appearance can make composite a discreet option, but the material still needs to suit the tooth and cavity. Your dentist can discuss how it may look and perform in your specific case.

Should I replace an intact amalgam filling with composite? A dentist generally doesn’t recommend replacing a sound filling for appearance or mercury concerns alone. Removal may affect healthy tooth structure, so ask your dentist to examine it and explain whether monitoring or treatment is needed.

How can I tell if a filling needs attention? A filling may need evaluation if it feels loose, chipped, or rough, or if you develop pain or sensitivity. Regular dental exams can also help your dentist spot wear or changes before they cause more trouble.

Will dental insurance cover the filling I prefer? Coverage varies by plan and may depend on the material and tooth being treated. Ask your insurer and dental office to review your benefits and provide an estimate of any expected out-of-pocket cost.
